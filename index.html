Why some meals feel like coming home

Comfort food isn't a category of ingredients — it's a category of memory. A look at what actually makes a dish comforting, and how the idea shows up differently around the world.

The underlying idea

Comfort food is defined by memory, not by nutrition label

Researchers who study eating behavior describe comfort food less by what's in it and more by what it's tied to: a specific person, place, or moment from earlier in life. A dish someone's grandmother made becomes comforting for that reason alone, regardless of whether it's a stew, a soup, or a plate of noodles.

That's why comfort food differs so much by household and country. There's no universal comfort dish — only a universal feeling that certain foods reliably produce.

What the studies find

The psychology behind the craving

Comfort eating is strongly linked to seeking familiarity during stress, loneliness, or change — the food acts as a stand-in for social connection. Studies on the topic have found that thinking about a comfort food can reduce feelings of loneliness in the same way recalling a supportive relationship does.

Texture and temperature matter too. Warm, soft, and slightly sweet or salty foods are consistently rated as more comforting than cold or crunchy ones — likely tied back to the softness of foods fed to us as infants and young children.

Three ingredients, roughly

What most comfort dishes have in common

Softness

Easy to eat

Mashed, stewed, or baked until tender — comfort dishes rarely ask much of the jaw. Low effort eating reads, subconsciously, as low effort care.

Warmth

Served hot

Heat is strongly associated with safety and shelter across cultures — one reason soups, stews, and baked dishes dominate comfort-food lists worldwide.

History

Tied to someone

The most comforting version of a dish is rarely the most technically refined one — it's the one closest to how it was first made for you.

A closer look

Why fat, salt, and starch keep showing up

Comfort dishes lean heavily on fat, salt, and refined carbohydrates for a physiological reason as much as a cultural one — these combinations trigger the brain's reward pathways more strongly than any single element alone, which is part of why mac and cheese, mashed potatoes, and fried dishes recur across so many different food cultures.

None of this makes comfort food "bad" — occasional, enjoyed food is part of a balanced relationship with eating. The dishes simply aren't designed around nutrition first; they're designed around feeling.

Same feeling, different plate

Comfort food around the world

Every food culture has its own version of "the dish you make when someone needs looking after."

Chicken soup

United States, Eastern Europe

Often the first thing made for someone who's sick or grieving — warm broth, soft noodles or rice, and a long simmer that fills the house with smell before the meal even starts.

Congee

China

Rice slow-cooked into a soft porridge, plain enough to eat when little else appeals — a staple comfort food for illness, infancy, and old age alike.

Khichdi

India

Rice and lentils cooked together into a soft, mild dish, often the first solid food given to babies and the go-to meal during recovery from illness.

Bangers and mash

United Kingdom

Sausages, mashed potato, and gravy — heavy, warm, and unfussy, built for cold weather rather than presentation.

Sancocho

Latin America & Caribbean

A hearty stew of meat, root vegetables, and plantain, often simmered for hours and served as a Sunday, family-gathering dish.

Okayu

Japan

A soft rice porridge, close to congee, associated with being cared for while unwell — plain rice cooked down until gentle on the stomach.

Same comfort, lighter build

Small adjustments that keep the feeling, change the makeup

Comfort food doesn't need to be avoided to eat well — a few common substitutions keep the texture and flavor people are actually craving while shifting the nutritional balance.

Mashed potatoes

Blend in roasted cauliflower with the potato for the same creamy texture with fewer refined carbs, using less butter to compensate.

Half potato, half cauliflower

Baked mac and cheese

A roux made with a bit less cheese, backed up with nutritional yeast or a sharper aged cheese, keeps the flavor with less fat overall.

Sharper cheese, smaller amount

Chicken soup

Loading the pot with extra vegetables and using shredded chicken breast keeps the broth just as rich while adding fiber and lean protein.

More vegetables, same broth

Fried chicken

A buttermilk marinade plus a hot oven or air fryer gets a similarly crisp coating with a fraction of the oil.

Oven-fried, same crust
Second thoughts

A few assumptions about comfort food, checked

"Comfort food is always unhealthy"
Plenty of traditional comfort dishes — chicken soup, khichdi, congee, vegetable stews — are simple, whole-food meals. The "unhealthy" reputation comes mostly from a narrower, heavily processed subset, not the category as a whole.
"Eating for comfort is a sign of poor willpower"
Seeking familiar food during stress is a well-documented, near-universal response tied to how the brain processes emotional regulation — not a personal failing. The concern is frequency and pattern, not the occasional instinct itself.
"Comfort food has to be indulgent to work"
What makes a dish comforting is its association with care and memory, not its calorie count — a plain bowl of rice porridge is, for many people, exactly as comforting as a dessert.
"Everyone's comfort food is roughly the same"
Because comfort is tied to personal history, it's one of the most individual categories of food there is — shaped entirely by what a specific household or culture made during childhood.
The point of it

Cooking as a form of care

What ties every comfort dish together, across every country and household, isn't an ingredient — it's the act of someone taking the time to make it. That's the part worth keeping, whichever version of the dish ends up on the table: comfort food, at its core, is just food made with someone specific in mind.
